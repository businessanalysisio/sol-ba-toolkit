# Excessive Self-Regard Tendency

## Overview

Excessive Self-Regard Tendency is the well-documented human habit of overestimating our own abilities, judgment, and the value of what we own. Most people rate themselves above average as drivers, employees, and decision-makers — a statistical impossibility in aggregate — and Kaufman ties this to related distortions like the endowment effect, where we value something more simply because it is ours. The tendency exists because a baseline of confidence was evolutionarily useful, but in business it quietly corrupts decisions: we trust our own untested plans, overvalue the products we built, dismiss competitors, and assume other people see our work as favorably as we do. The danger is not the confidence itself but the invisibility of it — the tendency feels like accurate self-assessment from the inside.

## Context & Usage

Watch for this whenever you are evaluating your own work, forecasting your own performance, or pricing something you created and have grown attached to. A founder convinced their product is obviously superior may skip real customer research, because the excessive self-regard makes validation feel unnecessary. The corrective is structural rather than willpower-based: seek disconfirming outside feedback, invite a devil's advocate, compare your predictions against recorded outcomes, and separate "how good I think this is" from "what evidence shows." When hiring or partnering, expect everyone (including yourself) to describe their own contributions generously, and discount accordingly. It compounds with Confirmation Bias (498), since an inflated view of your judgment makes you even quicker to dismiss evidence you were wrong.
