# Commander's Intent

## Overview

A delegation principle borrowed from military planning: when assigning a task, communicate the *purpose* behind it — the end state you want and why it matters — not just the steps. Battle plans rarely survive contact with reality, so officers state the intent ("hold this bridge so the supply line stays open") and trust subordinates to improvise when conditions change. Kaufman's point for business: if people know only the instructions, any surprise stalls them or produces literal-minded compliance; if they know the intent, they can adapt intelligently without checking back.

## Context & Usage

Use whenever you delegate anything non-trivial or write a brief someone else will execute. Instead of "call these ten customers and ask these five questions," say "we need to know why churn doubled in March — here are ten affected customers; find out what changed for them." The second version survives a customer going off-script. Commander's Intent is also the antidote to bottlenecked managers: teams that know the intent make ten small decisions a day that would otherwise queue up in your inbox.
