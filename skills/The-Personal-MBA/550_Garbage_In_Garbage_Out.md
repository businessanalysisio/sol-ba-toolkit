# Garbage In, Garbage Out

## Overview

The quality of what a process produces can never exceed the quality of what you feed into it — Garbage In, Garbage Out. Kaufman applies this borrowed computing maxim to analysis and decision-making: an elaborate model, a polished dashboard, or a confident forecast built on flawed data is worse than useless, because it launders bad inputs into conclusions that look authoritative and are trusted accordingly. The sophistication of the processing is irrelevant if the raw material is wrong. A revenue projection derived from sales figures that double-count returns will be precisely, professionally wrong; a customer segmentation drawn from a survey with a biased sample will confidently describe customers who don't exist. The danger is compounded by presentation — the more refined the output looks, the less anyone questions the inputs that produced it.

## Context & Usage

Before trusting any analysis, interrogate its inputs first: where did this data come from, how was it collected, what does it exclude, and could it be systematically skewed? Spend proportionate effort validating source data rather than polishing the analysis on top of it, since no amount of downstream rigor rescues a corrupt input. Be especially wary when a clean, confident result rests on data you didn't gather and can't inspect. This principle is why Measurement (548) has to be honest at the point of collection, and it pairs with Analytical Honesty (551): guarding against bad inputs and guarding against biased interpretation are the two ways analysis goes wrong.
