# Pricing Uncertainty Principle

## Overview

The Pricing Uncertainty Principle is Kaufman's blunt observation that all prices are arbitrary and malleable — there is no "true" or objectively correct price waiting to be discovered. Every price is a policy decision, a claim about value that the seller invents and the market either accepts or rejects. Because no natural law fixes what a thing must cost, the number you attach to an offer can always be defended, argued, raised, or lowered, and the same product can rationally sell at wildly different prices to different buyers. This is liberating rather than unsettling: since price is a decision, not a fact, you are free to set it deliberately and to test it, instead of anchoring to cost-plus habit or a competitor's tag.

## Context & Usage

Invoke this whenever fear or false precision is driving pricing — the founder who underprices because they cannot "prove" a higher number is justified, or the team paralyzed searching for the one right figure. The principle says to stop looking for objective validation and instead name a price, present it confidently, and read the market's response. Raising prices is a legitimate experiment: a consultant who has never lost a bid on price is almost certainly charging too little, and the only way to find the ceiling is to test it. Pair this with a chosen justification (see Four Pricing Methods, 410) so the number has a story behind it, but remember the story exists to persuade — it does not make the price any less a choice.
