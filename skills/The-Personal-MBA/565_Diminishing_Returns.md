# Diminishing Returns

## Overview

Diminishing Returns is the pattern where each additional unit of input eventually produces less additional output than the unit before it — the curve that rises steeply at first, then flattens. Kaufman pairs it with the Critical Few to complete the picture of where effort pays off: early investment in almost anything yields a lot (the first hour of testing, the first salesperson, the first round of cleanup), but as you keep adding, the marginal gain shrinks toward zero, and past a certain point can even go negative, where more input actively makes things worse. The tenth hour polishing a design that was already good enough buys almost nothing; hiring the fifth person into a role two could handle adds coordination cost that reduces total output. The point of the model is knowing where you are on that curve, so you keep investing while returns are rich and stop before you're pouring effort into a flat or falling stretch.

## Context & Usage

Invoke this whenever you're deciding how much of something to do — how much to spend, how long to refine, how many people to add, how far to optimize. The discipline is to find "good enough" and stop, redirecting the marginal effort to somewhere still on the steep part of its own curve, which is where the Critical Few (564) and Constraint (536) point you. It is the built-in stopping rule for Optimization (562): optimization without an awareness of diminishing returns runs past the point of value into wasted effort and, eventually, over-optimization that harms the system. Watch specifically for the negative-returns zone — perfectionism, overstaffing, over-engineering — where continued input isn't just wasteful but counterproductive.
