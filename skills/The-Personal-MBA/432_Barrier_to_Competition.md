# Barrier to Competition

## Overview

A Barrier to Competition is anything that makes it hard for rivals to copy your position and take your customers — the "moat" around a business. Kaufman notes that any profitable, easily copied business invites imitators who compete the profit away, so durable success depends on advantages that are difficult to replicate. Common barriers include proprietary technology or patents, accumulated assets like a large customer base or dataset, exclusive relationships and contracts, a trusted brand built over years, network effects where each new user makes the product more valuable, and sheer operational scale that lets you undercut newcomers. The strongest barriers tend to be ones that accumulate over time or strengthen with size, because a competitor can't buy them outright — they'd have to spend the same years or reach the same scale to match you.

## Context & Usage

Think about barriers to competition when your model is working and you need to protect the profits before others notice. Example: a marketplace that reaches critical mass gains a network-effect moat — buyers go there because sellers are there and vice versa — which a well-funded newcomer struggles to break, since an empty competing marketplace has nothing to offer either side. Audit your business for what actually stops a copycat: if the honest answer is "nothing," expect margins to erode and start building a defensible asset (brand, data, network, exclusive supply) deliberately. Barriers are rarely permanent, so treat them as something to keep reinforcing.
