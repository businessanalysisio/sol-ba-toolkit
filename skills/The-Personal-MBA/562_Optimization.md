# Optimization

## Overview

Optimization is the process of maximizing a system's performance on the output you care about — and Kaufman's central caution is that you must first decide what to optimize and where, because you can only optimize for one thing at a time and improving the wrong part yields nothing. The highest-leverage target is almost always the system's constraint (536): squeeze more from the bottleneck and total throughput rises, while polishing any non-constraint just accumulates work in front of the real limit. Equally important is that optimization has a shadow side — pushed too far, it trades away everything not being measured. A supply chain optimized purely for cost becomes fragile the first time a supplier fails, because the Slack (535) that would have absorbed the shock was optimized out. So the practical form of optimization is: find the constraint, improve it, and stop before the pursuit of one metric quietly destroys the resilience or quality you weren't counting.

## Context & Usage

Reach for optimization only after you've deconstructed the system and located what actually limits it, then concentrate effort there rather than spreading it evenly. Define the single objective explicitly and name what you're willing to trade for it, so the trade-offs are chosen rather than discovered later as damage. Watch for over-optimization, which shows up as brittleness, burnout, or eroded quality — signs that a local metric was maximized at the whole system's expense. And recognize when to stop: past a point, further optimization runs into Diminishing Returns (565), and the effort is better spent elsewhere. Optimization tunes an existing structure; when the structure itself is the problem, Refactoring (563) is the right tool instead.
