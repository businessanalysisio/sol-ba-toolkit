# Attachment

## Overview

Attachment is the mind's habit of fixing on a specific outcome, possession, or self-image and treating it as essential to one's wellbeing — and Kaufman flags it as a quiet source of both suffering and bad decisions. When you are attached to a particular result (this deal must close, this product must succeed, this plan must be right), the stakes feel existential, which floods the Onion Brain (459) with threat, narrows your thinking, and makes you cling to a losing course rather than adapt. Attachment also distorts valuation, as in the endowment effect where you overprice what is yours simply because it is yours. Non-attachment is not not-caring; it is caring about doing good work while holding the specific outcome loosely, so that you can respond to reality instead of defending a fixed picture of how things must go.

## Context & Usage

Watch for attachment when a decision feels emotionally charged out of proportion — sunk-cost persistence on a failing project, refusing a reasonable offer because you are anchored to a number, or defending an idea because it is "yours" rather than because it is right. The practical move is to separate your identity and equanimity from the outcome: commit fully to the effort and the process, then let go of your grip on the result, which paradoxically improves the result by keeping your judgment clear. A negotiator who can genuinely walk away (see Next Best Alternative, 413) negotiates better precisely because they are not attached to closing. Naming the attachment out loud ("I'm attached to being right here") often loosens it enough to think again. It works hand in hand with Locus of Control (505).
