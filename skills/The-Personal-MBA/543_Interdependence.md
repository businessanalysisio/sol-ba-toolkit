# Interdependence

## Overview

The parts of a system depend on one another, and Interdependence is Kaufman's reminder that you can never really do just one thing — every action ripples through the connections to other parts. Because the elements are linked, changing one reliably affects others in ways that are easy to overlook when you focus narrowly on the part you are touching. Cut the marketing budget and you don't just save money; you slow the lead flow that sales depends on, which shrinks the revenue that funds the very operations you were trying to protect. Speed up one stage of production and you may simply flood the next stage, which now becomes the constraint. This connectedness is also why systems can't be understood by examining pieces in isolation: the behavior that matters lives in the relationships between parts, not in the parts themselves.

## Context & Usage

Use this before making any change to one component of a business, by asking what else is connected to it and how they will respond. It is the discipline behind mapping dependencies before acting, and it guards against the classic error of locally optimizing one function in a way that harms the whole — a purchasing team that hits its cost target by buying cheaper inputs that raise defect rates downstream. When diagnosing a problem, trace the connections rather than blaming the part where the symptom appears, since the cause often sits somewhere it is linked to. It leads directly into Second-Order Effects (545), the downstream consequences these links produce.
