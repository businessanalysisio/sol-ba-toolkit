# Testing (self-experimentation)

## Overview

Testing, or self-experimentation, is the practice of learning what actually works by trying it in reality and observing the result, rather than relying on theory, opinion, or what should work in principle. Kaufman's stance is that you are a system too complex and too particular for general advice to reliably predict — what boosts one person's energy, focus, or output may do nothing for you — so the only trustworthy way to know is to run the experiment on yourself: change one variable, hold the rest steady, watch what happens, and keep what works. This turns self-improvement from a matter of belief into a matter of evidence, and it applies equally to business: assumptions about customers, prices, and processes are hypotheses until a real test confirms them.

## Context & Usage

Use it whenever you catch yourself arguing about what will happen instead of finding out. For personal habits, isolate one change — cut caffeine after noon, or move deep work to the morning — run it long enough to see an effect, and judge by results rather than by how the idea felt. In business, the same discipline yields cheap answers: rather than debating a price, run the higher price with a segment of customers and measure conversion and revenue. Because it produces real evidence, testing is the natural corrective to the biases in this chapter — Confirmation Bias (498) and Excessive Self-Regard Tendency (497) both dissolve against a well-designed test, and it keeps Counterfactual Simulation (494) honest by checking imagined outcomes against actual ones. Keep experiments small, reversible, and measured so a wrong guess costs little.
