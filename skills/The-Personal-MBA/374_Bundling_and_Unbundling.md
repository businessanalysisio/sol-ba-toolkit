# Bundling and Unbundling

## Overview

Bundling combines several separate offers into one package sold at a single price; unbundling splits a package into pieces sold individually. Both are ways to create new value and reach new customers from components you already have. Bundling works when the combined package is more attractive or convenient than buying parts separately — a cable provider selling hundreds of channels as one subscription, or a software suite bundling word processor, spreadsheet, and email. Unbundling works when customers want only part of what's on offer and resent paying for the rest — streaming services letting viewers pick individual shows, or à la carte pricing that peels apart a suite. Kaufman notes the two are perpetual opposites: markets swing between bundling and unbundling as customer preferences and technology shift, and there is money to be made riding the swing in either direction.

## Context & Usage

Apply this when looking for new revenue or defending against a competitor. If rivals win by selling a cheap, focused tool, you can bundle it into a broader package that offers convenience; if an incumbent forces customers to buy a bloated suite, you can unbundle the one feature people actually want and sell it alone. Streaming's unbundling of the cable package, followed by the re-bundling of many streaming services into new tiers, is the pattern in miniature. Modularity (373) is the enabler — you can only bundle and unbundle cleanly what was built as separable parts.
