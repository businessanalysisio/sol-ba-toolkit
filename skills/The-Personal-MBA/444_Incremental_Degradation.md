# Incremental Degradation

## Overview

Incremental Degradation is the slow erosion of quality or value caused by a series of small compromises, each too minor to notice on its own but ruinous in aggregate. Kaufman's warning is that businesses rarely collapse from one big bad decision; they decline from a thousand tiny ones — a slightly cheaper ingredient here, a marginally smaller portion there, a support process trimmed to save a little time, a standard relaxed just this once. Each individual cut seems harmless and saves a bit of money, so it's easy to justify, and because no single step is visible to customers, the feedback that would stop it never arrives. But the cuts compound, and eventually customers sense the product isn't what it was and drift away, often without being able to say exactly why. The danger is precisely that the process is gradual and invisible.

## Context & Usage

Watch for incremental degradation whenever cost-cutting becomes routine, especially under margin pressure. Example: a restaurant under financial strain swaps to cheaper cheese, then trims portions, then cuts a prep step — each move saves a little and passes unnoticed, until a year later regulars quietly stop coming because "it's just not as good anymore." Guard against it by defining the quality standards that are non-negotiable and reviewing the cumulative effect of small changes, not just each one in isolation. When trimming costs, ask not "does this one cut hurt?" but "where does this pattern of cuts lead?"
