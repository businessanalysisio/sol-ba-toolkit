# Segmentation

## Overview

Segmentation is splitting aggregated data into meaningful groups so that patterns the overall total hides come into view. Kaufman's point is that averages and grand totals blend distinct populations into a single figure that describes none of them accurately, and the insight usually lives in the differences between groups rather than in the lump sum. "Average revenue per customer is $80" can conceal that a small band of customers spends $500 while the majority spend $20 — a fact that changes everything about where you focus. Breaking the whole apart by attributes that matter (customer type, acquisition channel, region, cohort, time period) turns a flat number into a map of where value, cost, and problems actually concentrate. A flat conversion rate looks fine until segmentation reveals that mobile users convert at a third the rate of desktop, pinpointing exactly where to act.

## Context & Usage

Segment whenever an aggregate number is guiding a decision, because the average almost always smears over variation you need to see. Slice by the dimensions plausibly linked to what you're investigating — which channels bring profitable versus unprofitable customers, which cohorts churn, which product lines carry the margin. It's the analytical partner of Deconstruction (547): one breaks a process into steps, the other breaks a population into groups, and both defeat the misleading single summary. Guard against over-segmenting, though — cut the data too finely and each group becomes too small to say anything reliable about (see Sampling, 553, and Confidence Interval, 554). Segmentation is also what exposes the Critical Few (564) hiding inside a total.
