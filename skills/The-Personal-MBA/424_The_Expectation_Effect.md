# The Expectation Effect

## Overview

The Expectation Effect captures the fact that customer satisfaction is not absolute — it is the gap between what a customer expected and what they actually experienced. The same delivery can delight or disappoint depending purely on the expectation you set beforehand. Kaufman's practical takeaway is that expectations are largely under your control, so managing them is a core part of delivering value: promise a two-day fix and deliver in one, and the customer is thrilled; promise same-day and deliver next-day, and the identical outcome feels like a failure. This is the mechanism behind "under-promise and over-deliver." The danger runs both ways — inflated marketing claims raise expectations to a level real delivery can't meet, manufacturing dissatisfaction from perfectly good work.

## Context & Usage

Manage expectations at every point where you make a promise — sales copy, delivery estimates, onboarding, support SLAs. Example: a contractor who quotes "done in three weeks" and finishes in two earns a referral, while a competitor who promised one week and took two, doing the same quality work, gets a bad review. Set expectations slightly conservative where you can, and if you can't hit a promise, reset the expectation proactively before the deadline rather than after. Because perception is relative, deliberately shaping expectations is often cheaper than improving the underlying delivery.
