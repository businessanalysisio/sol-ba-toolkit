# Measurement

## Overview

Measurement is the act of collecting data about a system so you can actually see what it is doing, and Kaufman's governing principle is that you can't reliably improve what you don't measure. Without numbers you are steering on impression and memory, both of which are systematically biased toward what you want to be true; with even a rough count you can tell whether a change helped or hurt. He is pragmatic about it — measurement need not be elaborate or precise to be useful, and a crude number tracked consistently beats a perfect one you never collect. The purpose is decision support, not accumulation: a measurement earns its keep only if it would change what you do. Knowing your conversion rate is valuable because it tells you whether a new landing page is worth keeping; a metric that no possible value of it would make you act on is just noise you pay to gather.

## Context & Usage

Measure before and after any change you make, so you can distinguish real effects from wishful thinking, and measure the few things tied to decisions you actually face rather than everything you could count. Start crude and refine only if the decision warrants it. Beware the failure modes that follow: measuring the easy proxy instead of the thing you care about (see Proxy, 559), and measuring so much that the signal drowns. Measurement is the foundation the rest of the Analyzing Systems toolkit stands on — you cannot compute a ratio, spot a correlation, or pick a KPI without first collecting honest data.
