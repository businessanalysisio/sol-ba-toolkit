# Caveman Syndrome

## Overview

Caveman Syndrome is Kaufman's name for the mismatch between our Paleolithic-era wiring and the modern world we now inhabit. The human body and brain were shaped over hundreds of thousands of years for a hunter-gatherer environment of scarce food, physical activity, small tribes, and immediate physical threats — and that hardware did not update when civilization arrived a few thousand years ago. The result is a long list of predictable malfunctions: we crave fat, salt, and sugar that were once rare and are now everywhere, so we overeat; we react to a critical email with the same stress-hormone cascade evolved for a charging predator; we sit still all day in bodies built to move. Recognizing these are ancient programs running in a novel context, not personal failings, is the first step to working around them.

## Context & Usage

Use this lens to explain otherwise puzzling behavior — your own and other people's — and to design environments that fit human wiring rather than fight it. Because willpower rarely beats an evolved drive head-on, the practical move is to change the surroundings: if you keep junk food out of the house, the ancient sugar-craving has nothing to act on. Understanding the caveman stress response tells a manager that a barrage of "urgent" alerts keeps a team in a low-grade fight-or-flight state that degrades thinking, so batching communication is not just tidy but biologically kinder. The syndrome underlies several later models — the finite energy of The Gas Tank (458) and the threat-driven Onion Brain (459) are both consequences of the same outdated hardware.
