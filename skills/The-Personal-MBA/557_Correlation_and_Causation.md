# Correlation and Causation

## Overview

Two things moving together does not mean one causes the other — correlation does not imply causation, the error Kaufman flags as among the most expensive in business analysis. When two variables rise and fall in step, there are always several explanations besides "A causes B": B might cause A (reverse causation), some third factor might drive both (a confounder), or the pattern might be pure coincidence dredged up by looking at enough variables. Ice cream sales and drownings correlate strongly, but neither causes the other — hot weather drives both. The business version is costly because it prompts action: a company notices that customers who use a certain feature retain better and rushes to push everyone toward the feature, when in truth the already-committed customers were both more likely to use it and more likely to stay, so forcing the feature changes nothing.

## Context & Usage

Whenever a correlation tempts you to act — "users who do X stay longer, so let's make everyone do X" — stop and ask whether X actually causes the outcome, whether the outcome causes X, or whether a hidden third factor produces both. The only reliable way to establish causation is a controlled experiment: change X for a randomly chosen group, hold everything else equal, and see whether the outcome moves. Treat observational correlations as hypotheses worth testing, not conclusions worth betting on. This discipline is central to Analytical Honesty (551) and depends on the sampling and confidence-interval reasoning (553, 554) needed to tell a real effect from noise.
