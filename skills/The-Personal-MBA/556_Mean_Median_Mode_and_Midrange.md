# Mean, Median, Mode, and Midrange

## Overview

"Average" is not one number but four, and Kaufman lays them out because choosing the wrong one — or letting someone else choose it for you — quietly distorts almost every summary of data. The mean is the arithmetic average (sum ÷ count) and is dragged around by outliers. The median is the middle value when the data is sorted, unmoved by extremes. The mode is the most frequently occurring value. The midrange is simply the highest and lowest averaged together. On symmetric data they roughly agree; on skewed data they diverge sharply, and that gap is where misinterpretation lives. In an office where the founder earns $1M and nine staff earn $40,000, the mean salary is $136,000 while the median is $40,000 — the median describes what a typical person actually earns, and the mean describes almost no one, yet either can be reported as "the average."

## Context & Usage

Whenever you see or cite an "average," ask which one it is and whether the data is skewed enough for the choice to matter. For anything with a long tail — incomes, deal sizes, page views, response times — the median usually describes typical experience better than the mean, while the mean matters when totals do (mean order value times order count gives revenue). Watch for the rhetorical trick of picking whichever average flatters the story. Looking at more than one at once is itself informative: a large gap between mean and median is a fast signal that outliers are present and that a single summary statistic is hiding an uneven distribution worth investigating (see Segmentation, 560).
