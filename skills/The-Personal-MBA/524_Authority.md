# Authority

## Overview

People defer to perceived experts and credentials, often without examining the substance behind them — the tendency Milgram's obedience experiments demonstrated at its most extreme, where ordinary people administered what they believed were dangerous shocks because a man in a lab coat said to continue. The markers that trigger deference are surprisingly shallow: titles, uniforms, jargon, media mentions, association with respected institutions. Authority is thus both a legitimate shortcut (real experts save us evaluation effort) and a systematic vulnerability.

## Context & Usage

Two directions of use. Building authority: if you sell expertise, accumulate and display credible markers — published work, speaking, certifications, recognizable client names, precise domain vocabulary — because prospects cannot directly evaluate your competence and will use these proxies instead. Defending against authority: when receiving advice, separate the credential from the argument; ask what the expert's incentives are (see Incentive-Caused Bias) and whether their expertise actually covers this question — a famous economist's stock picks carry no special weight. In meetings, note when a room defers to the highest-paid person's opinion rather than the best-supported one.
