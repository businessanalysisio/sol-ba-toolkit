# Buffer

## Overview

A Buffer is a third party you empower to negotiate on your behalf — an agent, broker, lawyer, or business manager who stands between you and the other side. Kaufman's point is that the buffer buys you two things that are hard to get when you represent yourself: emotional distance and time. Because the buffer is not personally invested, they can push harder, say no more cleanly, and avoid the flinch that gives away your real position. The buffer also has a built-in delay — "I'll have to check with my client" — which lets you deliberate instead of reacting in the moment. The classic example is a real-estate or literary agent: even after paying the commission, sellers often net more because the agent negotiates without the owner's emotional attachment clouding the deal. The trade-offs are cost and a slight loss of direct control, so buffers pay off most on high-stakes or emotionally charged transactions.

## Context & Usage

Use a buffer when the deal matters a great deal to you personally and that attachment would weaken your position — selling a company you founded, a salary negotiation where you fear straining a relationship, or a purchase where you might overpay out of excitement. Example: a founder selling their business hires a corporate-finance advisor so the hard price pushes come from the advisor, not from the founder who must keep working with the buyer through an earn-out. Be explicit with your buffer about your walk-away point and the authority you are delegating, so they can protect it without checking every move.
