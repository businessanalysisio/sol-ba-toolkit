# Return on Investment (ROI)

## Overview

Return on Investment measures how much you gain relative to what you put in: (gain from the investment − cost of the investment) ÷ cost, usually expressed as a percentage. Kaufman treats ROI as the common yardstick for comparing dissimilar uses of money, because it normalizes results to the size of the outlay — a $1,000 profit means very different things on a $2,000 investment (50% ROI) versus a $50,000 one (2% ROI). Reducing every option to a rate of return lets you rank an ad campaign against a new hire against a piece of equipment on the same scale. The caveats Kaufman stresses are that ROI is only as good as its inputs — inflated benefit estimates or ignored costs produce flattering but false numbers — and that it should be read alongside time (a 20% return in a month beats 20% over a year) and risk (a high expected ROI on a shaky bet may be worse than a modest sure thing).

## Context & Usage

Use ROI to compare and prioritize investments competing for the same limited money. Example: a marketer weighing three channels computes the return of each — a channel returning 300% clearly outranks one returning 40% — and shifts the budget toward the highest performers. Be honest about all the costs (including time) and skeptical of rosy benefit projections, and always factor in how long the return takes and how certain it is. ROI pairs naturally with opportunity cost: the best investment is the one whose return beats every realistic alternative for the same resources.
