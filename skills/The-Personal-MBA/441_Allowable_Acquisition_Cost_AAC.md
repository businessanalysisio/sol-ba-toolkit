# Allowable Acquisition Cost (AAC)

## Overview

Allowable Acquisition Cost is the maximum you can afford to spend to win a new customer while still coming out ahead, given what that customer is worth to you over time. It is set directly by lifetime value: as long as your acquisition cost stays below the customer's LTV, each new customer is profitable; once acquisition cost exceeds LTV, you lose money on every sale no matter how fast you grow. Kaufman highlights AAC because it converts marketing from a guessing game into arithmetic — if a customer is worth $168 in lifetime profit, you can rationally spend up to (some margin below) that $168 on ads, sales effort, and offers to acquire them. Knowing your AAC lets you outbid competitors for attention where it pays, and stop spending where it doesn't. The trap it prevents is the common startup failure of paying more to acquire customers than those customers will ever be worth.

## Context & Usage

Calculate AAC from LTV before scaling any paid acquisition channel. Example: a company with a $168 LTV and a target profit margin decides it can spend up to $60 to acquire a customer; a channel delivering customers at $40 is worth scaling hard, while one costing $90 is quietly bankrupting the business and must be cut. Use AAC as the go/no-go line for each channel and campaign, and recompute it whenever LTV shifts (better retention raises AAC and lets you spend more to grow). The relationship is the core discipline: acquisition cost must stay comfortably below lifetime value, with the gap being your profit.
