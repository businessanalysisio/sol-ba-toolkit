# Hierarchy of Funding

## Overview

The Hierarchy of Funding is the ordered menu of ways to finance a business, ranked roughly from cheapest and least-restrictive to most costly and most controlling. Kaufman's ladder runs, in effect: your own money and reinvested revenue first (no strings, no dilution), then customer prepayments and trade credit, then loans and debt (you keep ownership but owe repayment with interest), and finally outside equity from investors (money with no repayment obligation but at the price of a permanent share of ownership and control). The core insight is that funding sources are not interchangeable — each carries a different cost, not just in interest or equity given up, but in obligations, control, and constraints imposed on how you run the business. Cheaper sources higher on the ladder preserve your autonomy; the further down you reach, the more of the business's future and independence you trade away for capital now.

## Context & Usage

Consult the hierarchy before raising money, working from the top down. Example: a founder needing capital first tries funding growth from revenue and customer deposits, then a modest bank loan, and only turns to venture equity when the opportunity genuinely can't be funded any other way — because taking investor money means giving up ownership and answering to shareholders forever. Exhaust the cheaper, less-restrictive options before descending, and weigh each source's full cost (control and obligations, not just price). The default should be the highest rung that can actually meet the need, since every step down surrenders more independence.
