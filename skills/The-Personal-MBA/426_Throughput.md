# Throughput

## Overview

Throughput is the rate at which a system produces its intended result — units shipped per day, customers served per hour, revenue generated per month. Borrowing from Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, Kaufman stresses that a system's throughput is governed by its single tightest bottleneck: the slowest step sets the pace for everything, no matter how fast the other steps run. Improving a non-bottleneck step doesn't raise throughput at all — it just piles up inventory or idle work in front of the real constraint. The way to increase throughput is therefore to find the bottleneck, relieve it, and then find the next one, because the constraint always moves once the first is fixed. Throughput measured against time is what actually turns activity into results; a busy system with low throughput is producing motion, not output.

## Context & Usage

Use throughput thinking whenever you want more output from an existing process and are tempted to speed up everything at once. Example: a bakery wanting to fill more orders discovers the oven, not the mixing or packing, is the bottleneck — so adding a second oven raises daily output, while hiring more packers would have changed nothing. Measure the flow rate, locate the constraint (work usually queues just before it), and direct all improvement there first. Once relieved, re-measure, because the limiting step will have shifted somewhere else.
