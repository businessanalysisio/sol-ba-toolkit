# Counterfactual Simulation

## Overview

Counterfactual Simulation is the mind's ability to run "what if" scenarios — imagining situations that have not happened and predicting how they would play out — without paying the cost of actually trying them. It is mental time travel: you construct a hypothetical ("What would happen if I doubled the price?") and your brain generates a plausible outcome by drawing on past experience. Kaufman flags this as one of the most valuable capacities we have, since it lets us test decisions in imagination and avoid costly mistakes in reality. But he is equally clear about its limits: a simulation is only a model built from what you already know, so it inherits every gap and bias in your experience, and it grows less reliable the further the scenario departs from anything you have seen. The map is not the territory.

## Context & Usage

Use it to pressure-test plans and decisions before committing resources — walk mentally through a launch, a hard conversation, or a "pre-mortem" in which you imagine the project has failed and ask why. This surfaces risks and second-order effects cheaply. The critical discipline is to trust the simulation only as far as your knowledge supports it: for novel territory, a vivid mental movie can feel like certainty while being mostly fiction, which is why founders confidently simulate a market response that the real market ignores. The corrective is to treat simulations as hypotheses and then actually test them (see Testing / self-experimentation, 503) rather than acting as though the imagined outcome were fact.
