# Hindsight Bias

## Overview

Hindsight Bias is the "I knew it all along" effect — once we know how something turned out, we perceive that outcome as having been far more predictable than it actually was beforehand. After a startup succeeds, its rise looks inevitable; after it fails, the warning signs seem to have been obvious. Kaufman warns that this retrospective distortion is dangerous precisely because it feels like wisdom: by rewriting our memory of what we expected, it convinces us the world is more predictable than it is, inflates our confidence in future forecasts, and corrupts the lessons we try to draw from experience. We end up "learning" that we should have seen the result coming, when in truth the information available at the time genuinely did not point clearly to it.

## Context & Usage

Beware this most during post-mortems and performance reviews, where it does real damage — blaming a decision-maker for not foreseeing an outcome that was unforeseeable given what they knew, or crediting luck as skill. The antidote is to judge decisions by the quality of reasoning and information available at the moment they were made, not by how they happened to turn out. Keep a decision journal: write down, in advance, what you predict and why, so later you can compare your actual prior expectation against the outcome instead of a rewritten memory. When reviewing a failure, ask "what did we know then?" rather than "how did we miss the obvious?" This protects the learning loop that Excessive Self-Regard Tendency (497) and Confirmation Bias (498) already threaten.
