# Counterparty Risk

## Overview

Whenever you depend on another party to fulfill an obligation, you carry Counterparty Risk — the chance that they fail to deliver, whether through incompetence, insolvency, dishonesty, or plain bad luck. Kaufman's point is that this risk is unavoidable in any business, because business is a web of promises: the supplier who will ship on time, the customer who will pay the invoice, the bank that will honor the deposit, the partner who will hold up their end. Every one of those relationships is a place where someone else's failure becomes your problem. The exposure grows with concentration — a firm that depends on a single supplier or one dominant customer has bet its survival on that counterparty's reliability, so when they falter, as when a key distributor goes bankrupt owing months of receivables, the loss can be existential rather than merely inconvenient.

## Context & Usage

Map your counterparty risk deliberately: list the outside parties whose failure would seriously hurt you, and weight them by both likelihood and impact. Reduce concentration where you can — diversify suppliers, broaden the customer base, avoid a single point of financial dependence — and manage the residual with deposits, staged payments, escrow, contracts, credit checks, and reputation vetting before extending trust. The practical rule is to never let any one counterparty become large enough that their failure would take you down. It connects to Stock (534) and Slack (535): reserves are partly what let you survive a counterparty who lets you down.
