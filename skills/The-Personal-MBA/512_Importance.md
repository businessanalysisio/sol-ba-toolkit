# Importance

## Overview

One of the deepest and most persistent human cravings is the desire to feel important — to be seen, valued, and treated as someone whose presence and contribution matter. Kaufman treats this as a lever for working with others: the fastest way to earn goodwill, cooperation, and discretionary effort is to make the people around you genuinely feel important, and the fastest way to lose them is to make them feel small, ignored, or interchangeable. The craving is real whether or not it is rational, so it pays to honor it directly. Concretely, a manager who publicly credits the team member whose idea shaped a decision gets more energy and loyalty from that person than one who quietly absorbs the idea as their own — the recognition, not the money, is what moves the relationship. This is not flattery, which people detect and resent; it is the habit of noticing real contributions and acknowledging them where it counts.

## Context & Usage

Reach for this whenever you need something from another person that you cannot command — a favor from a peer, effort from a volunteer, buy-in from a stakeholder with no reporting line to you. Before asking, ask yourself what would make this person feel that helping you is worth their time and reflects well on them. In practice: address people by name, ask for their view before offering yours, attribute wins publicly and absorb blame privately, and frame requests around the value only they can add. It also works as a diagnostic — when someone is quietly obstructing a project, the root cause is often that a decision made them feel overlooked, and repairing that is cheaper than escalating.
