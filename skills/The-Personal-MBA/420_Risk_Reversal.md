# Risk Reversal

## Overview

Risk Reversal is a strategy that shifts the perceived risk of a transaction from the buyer onto the seller, dissolving the fear of loss that stops purchases. The canonical form is the money-back guarantee: if the product doesn't deliver, the buyer gets their money back, so the worst case for the buyer becomes "no harm done." Kaufman notes that most prospects hesitate not because they doubt the value but because they fear being the fool who wasted money — and reversing that risk directly attacks the "it won't work" and "it won't work for me" barriers to purchase. Counterintuitively, stronger guarantees usually increase net profit: the sales lift from removed hesitation outweighs the small fraction who claim refunds, and a bold guarantee also signals real confidence in the offer. Variations include free trials, "pay only if it works," and warranties that extend the reversal over time.

## Context & Usage

Add risk reversal whenever fear of loss, not lack of interest, is throttling sales — especially for higher-priced or unfamiliar offers. Example: a training course lifts enrollments sharply by replacing "no refunds" with "complete the first module; if it's not worth it, full refund, no questions," letting fence-sitters commit. Make the guarantee specific and easy to invoke, because a hedged or hard-to-claim guarantee reverses little risk. Track refund rates against the sales lift to confirm the reversal is net-positive; if refunds spike, the problem is usually the product, not the guarantee.
