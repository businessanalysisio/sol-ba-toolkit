# Normal Accidents

## Overview

In systems that are both complex and tightly coupled, catastrophic failures are not freak events but a normal, expected feature of how the system works — this is Charles Perrow's finding, which Kaufman adopts under the name Normal Accidents. Complexity means many parts interact in ways no one fully understands; tight coupling means a failure in one part propagates fast, with little slack to absorb it before it hits the next. Put those together and small, individually harmless faults can interact in unforeseeable combinations to produce a disaster that no single point of failure explains — the pattern behind Three Mile Island, and behind cascading outages where a minor glitch, an automated response, and a maintenance window line up at the wrong moment. The unsettling implication is that you cannot fully engineer these accidents away; adding more safety systems adds more complexity, which can create new interaction paths of its own.

## Context & Usage

Expect this in any operation that is intricate and tightly linked — automated trading, complex supply chains, sprawling software with many integrations, chemical or industrial processes. Since you can't eliminate the accidents, reduce your exposure to them: loosen coupling by adding buffers and Slack (535) between stages, simplify where you can (Gall's Law, 532), build in circuit breakers that stop cascades, and rehearse failure so the response is practiced. Above all, design so that when something fails — and it will — the failure is contained and survivable rather than system-ending. It is the risk-side complement to Interdependence (543) and Second-Order Effects (545).
