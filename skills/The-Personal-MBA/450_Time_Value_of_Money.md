# Time Value of Money

## Overview

The Time Value of Money is the principle that a dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future. Kaufman explains why: money you have now can be invested to earn a return, inflation erodes future purchasing power, and there's always risk that a promised future payment never arrives. A dollar in hand today, put to work, becomes more than a dollar later — so future money must be discounted to compare it fairly with present money. This is the logic behind discounting and net present value: $1,000 promised in three years is worth noticeably less than $1,000 now, and how much less depends on the return you could earn in the meantime. The concept reshapes how you evaluate any deal involving time — a smaller sum sooner can beat a larger sum later, and money that arrives faster is genuinely more valuable even if the totals look equal on paper.

## Context & Usage

Apply the time value of money whenever cash flows are spread across time — comparing payment schedules, evaluating investments, or choosing between a lump sum and installments. Example: offered $10,000 now or $11,000 in two years, you weigh whether the extra $1,000 beats what you could earn investing the $10,000 over those two years; if you can reliably earn more than ~5% a year, taking the money now wins. Use it to favor faster payback and to discount rosy long-dated projections. It also explains why getting paid sooner (and paying later) has real financial value beyond mere convenience.
