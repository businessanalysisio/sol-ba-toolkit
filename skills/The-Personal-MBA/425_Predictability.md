# Predictability

## Overview

Predictability is the degree to which customers can rely on getting a consistent result from you every time. Kaufman argues that reliability is itself a form of value — often a larger one than peak quality. A fast-food chain does not serve the best meal in town, but it serves the exact same meal in a thousand locations, and that certainty is precisely what customers pay for when they're hungry in an unfamiliar city. Predictability lowers the customer's perceived risk (they know what they're getting) and reduces the mental effort of choosing again. It applies to delivery times, product quality, service tone, and pricing. The flip side is that inconsistency quietly destroys trust: a business that is occasionally excellent but often mediocre feels worse than one that is reliably good, because customers can't depend on it.

## Context & Usage

Prioritize predictability when repeat business and trust matter more than one-off brilliance — which is most businesses. Example: a coffee chain enforces standardized recipes and training so a latte tastes identical in every store, making customers comfortable walking in anywhere; the value is the sameness, not novelty. Build predictability through documented processes and systemization so the output doesn't depend on which person happens to be working that day. When you do vary the experience, do it in ways that only add upside, so the reliable baseline is never at risk.
