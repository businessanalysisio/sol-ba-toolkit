# Power

## Overview

Power, in Kaufman's definition, is simply the ability to influence the behavior of other people — to get things to happen through others. He strips the word of its sinister connotations: power is neutral, a capacity that can be used well or badly, and building it is a legitimate and necessary part of getting anything meaningful done through cooperation. Power accumulates from concrete sources — the value you can offer others, the trust and reputation you have earned (407), the relationships you maintain, the resources you control, and the skills that make you worth listening to. Crucially, the most durable power is not coercive but earned: people follow those who reliably create value for them, so the surest route to influence is becoming genuinely useful to others rather than trying to dominate them.

## Context & Usage

Think in these terms whenever you need to accomplish something that requires other people's cooperation — leading a team, winning a client, rallying partners. Rather than lamenting a lack of formal authority, inventory your actual sources of influence and build the ones you can: deliver value consistently, keep your promises so trust compounds, and cultivate relationships before you need them. A junior employee with no title can still wield real power by becoming the person whose judgment colleagues seek and whose help they rely on. Beware power built purely on coercion or leverage — it evaporates the moment the leverage does and breeds resentment that undermines it. Power built on earned trust and demonstrated value is far more stable. It underlies effective work through others alongside Comparative Advantage (510) and Communication Overhead (511).
