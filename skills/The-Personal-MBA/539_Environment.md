# Environment

## Overview

No system operates in a vacuum; it sits inside an Environment — the surrounding conditions, structures, and incentives that shape what the system does — and Kaufman's point is that the environment often determines behavior more powerfully than the intentions of the people inside it. You frequently cannot control a system's behavior directly, but you can change its environment, and the behavior follows. Put a bowl of fruit on the counter and hide the cookies and people eat more fruit, with no change in willpower required; the same logic scales to business, where a poorly laid-out warehouse guarantees slow picking no matter how motivated the staff, and a compensation plan that rewards volume produces volume even when the stated goal is quality. Because environments are usually inherited rather than designed, their effects are easy to miss and easy to blame on individuals instead.

## Context & Usage

When a system keeps producing an unwanted result despite good people trying hard, stop trying to change the people and change the environment that is shaping them — redesign the layout, rewrite the incentives, remove the friction, restructure the defaults. This reframes many "people problems" as design problems: if the easy path leads to the wrong outcome, make the right outcome the easy path. Use it proactively too, by designing environments that make desired behavior the default so you are not relying on constant discipline or supervision. Note the tie to Selection Test (540): the environment is what applies selective pressure, so it decides what behavior survives.
