# Leverage

## Overview

Leverage, in the financial sense, is using borrowed resources — most often debt — to amplify the size of what you can do beyond what your own capital allows. Kaufman's key caution is that leverage magnifies outcomes in both directions: borrowing to invest multiplies your gains if things go well, but multiplies your losses just as fiercely if they don't, and unlike your own money, borrowed money must be repaid regardless of how the bet turns out. Buy a $500,000 property with $100,000 of your own money and $400,000 of debt, and a 10% rise in value ($50,000) is a 50% return on your cash — but a 10% fall is a 50% loss, and you still owe the full loan. This asymmetry is why leverage is powerful and dangerous at once. Used carefully against reliable returns it accelerates growth; used carelessly it turns a manageable setback into ruin.

## Context & Usage

Consider leverage when a reliable opportunity exceeds your available capital and the expected return comfortably beats the cost of borrowing. Example: a business borrows to buy equipment that will clearly generate more than the loan's interest, using leverage to grow faster than reinvested profits alone would allow. The discipline is to leverage only where returns are dependable and to keep the obligation within what you could survive if things go wrong, because debt removes the option of simply waiting out a bad period. Match the risk of the underlying bet to the fixed, unforgiving nature of the repayment.
