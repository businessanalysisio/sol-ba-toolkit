# Comparative Advantage

## Overview

Comparative Advantage, borrowed from economics, is the principle that you gain the most by focusing on what you do relatively best and delegating or trading away the rest — even in cases where you could do the other tasks better than the person you hand them to. The counterintuitive core, which trips up most operators, is that the decisive comparison is not "am I better than them at this?" but "is this the highest-value use of my limited time?" If an hour of your attention is worth far more applied to your strongest skill, then handing off a task you happen to be good at frees that hour for where your advantage is greatest, and the total output rises for everyone. Kaufman uses this to argue against the founder's instinct to do everything personally simply because they can.

## Context & Usage

Apply this whenever you are deciding what to keep and what to delegate, outsource, or drop. List where your time produces disproportionate value — the work only you can do, or that you do far better than anyone available — and ruthlessly offload the rest, even competent, enjoyable tasks, because holding onto them starves your true advantage of time. A founder who is a brilliant salesperson but also a decent bookkeeper should hire the bookkeeping out: every hour on the books is an hour not selling, where their comparative advantage is largest. The same logic guides team design — assign each person to their comparative advantage rather than to whatever they can merely handle. It pairs with Power (509) and reduces the load that drives Communication Overhead (511) when work is well-allocated.
