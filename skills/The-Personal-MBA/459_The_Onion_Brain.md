# The Onion Brain

## Overview

The Onion Brain is Kaufman's shorthand for the brain's layered structure, built up in evolutionary stages like the rings of an onion. At the core sits the hindbrain — the oldest "lizard brain" that runs automatic survival functions and the fight-flight-or-freeze response. Wrapped around it is the midbrain, the seat of emotion, memory, and reward. Outermost is the forebrain, the recently evolved neocortex responsible for language, planning, and abstract reasoning. The decisive point is precedence: when the ancient survival layers detect a threat, they can hijack control from the rational forebrain, which is why a person who is angry or afraid literally cannot think clearly. Reason is a thin, easily overridden shell over much older machinery.

## Context & Usage

Use this to understand why smart people make terrible decisions under threat, and to design around it. In a high-stakes negotiation or heated meeting, if someone feels attacked their hindbrain takes over and productive reasoning stops — so the practical move is to lower the perceived threat (acknowledge their concern, slow down, break for a moment) before expecting rational discussion to resume. It also explains why fear-based marketing grabs attention: it speaks straight to the survival layer. For your own decisions, the rule is to avoid committing to anything important while emotionally activated; give the forebrain time to come back online. This layered model underpins Caveman Syndrome (457) and connects to how threats trigger the physical stress response.
