# Compounding

## Overview

Compounding is growth that feeds on itself — when the returns you earn generate their own returns, producing acceleration rather than a straight line. Kaufman highlights it as one of the most powerful and most underestimated forces in finance and beyond, because human intuition expects linear growth and badly underrates exponential growth. Money invested at a steady rate doesn't just add the same amount each period; each period's gain becomes part of the base that grows next period, so the curve bends steeply upward over time. The two levers that matter most are the rate and, especially, the time: small differences in rate or a few extra years of compounding produce enormous differences at the end, which is why starting early beats starting big. Compounding isn't limited to money — skills, reputation, audiences, and relationships compound too, with early consistent investment paying off disproportionately later.

## Context & Usage

Think in compounding terms for any long-horizon decision where gains can be reinvested. Example: reinvesting a business's profits back into growth rather than taking them all out means next year's profit grows from a larger base, and over a decade the reinvesting business dwarfs one of identical starting size that spent its earnings each year. Prioritize things that compound (retained earnings, accumulating assets, skills) and give them time, since the steep part of the curve arrives late. The practical lesson is patience plus consistency: the earlier and longer you let something compound, the more the back-loaded payoff rewards you.
