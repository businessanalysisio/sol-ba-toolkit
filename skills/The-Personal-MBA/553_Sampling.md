# Sampling

## Overview

You rarely can or need to examine every case, so Sampling is the practice of studying a subset to draw conclusions about the whole. Kaufman treats it as both a gift and a trap: it makes analysis affordable — you don't have to survey all your customers to learn what customers think — but the entire method rests on one condition, that the sample actually represents the population you care about. A biased sample produces confident conclusions about a group that doesn't exist. Ask only your happiest customers what they think of the product and you'll "discover" everyone loves it; judge demand from the people who already walked into your store and you've excluded everyone who didn't. Sample size matters too — three responses tell you almost nothing no matter how they were chosen — but representativeness is the deeper issue, because a large biased sample is still just a precise measurement of the wrong thing.

## Context & Usage

Use sampling whenever measuring everything is impractical, which is most of the time — surveys, quality checks, spot audits, user tests. Before trusting the result, ask two questions: who or what got excluded from this sample, and could that exclusion skew the answer in a predictable direction? Watch for self-selection (only motivated people respond), survivorship (you only see the cases that made it this far), and convenience sampling (you measured whoever was easy to reach). When it matters, sample randomly from the full population and gather enough cases to be meaningful. Sampling feeds directly into Confidence Interval (554), which quantifies how much the inevitable sampling error should temper your certainty.
