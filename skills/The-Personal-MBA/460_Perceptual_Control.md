# Perceptual Control

## Overview

Perceptual Control, drawn from William Powers' Perceptual Control Theory, holds that we do not directly control our behavior — we act in order to control our perceptions, keeping them matched to an internal standard. The canonical analogy is a thermostat: it does not "decide" to run the furnace, it continuously compares the room's actual temperature to its set point and switches heating on or off to close any gap. People work the same way. You have a perception you want to maintain (feeling safe, respected, well-fed, in control), and your actions are the varying means by which you keep the perceived reality near that target, adjusting automatically as circumstances push it off. Behavior, in this view, is the control of perception, not an end in itself.

## Context & Usage

This model reframes how you influence others: to change someone's behavior durably, you address the perception they are trying to control, not the surface action. An employee who keeps padding estimates may be controlling a perception of "not being blamed for missing deadlines" — attack the padding directly and it reappears elsewhere, but change the reference (make it safe to give honest estimates) and the behavior changes on its own. It also explains resistance: when your intervention pushes someone's perception away from their set point, they will act to counteract you, which feels like stubbornness but is just a control loop doing its job. Diagnose by asking what perception a person is defending, then work with that loop rather than against it. Behavior change runs through the Reference Level (461).
