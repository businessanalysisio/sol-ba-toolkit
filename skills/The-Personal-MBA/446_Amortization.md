# Amortization

## Overview

Amortization is spreading the cost of a large, one-time purchase across all the units it will help produce or the time it will serve, rather than charging it all against the moment you paid. Kaufman uses it to correct a common distortion: a big upfront expense looks alarming as a lump sum but often looks trivial once divided across its useful life. A $12,000 machine that lasts three years and produces 36,000 units costs roughly $0.33 of amortized cost per unit — a figure that lets you price and judge profitability sanely, whereas fixating on the $12,000 sticker might scare you off a good investment. The same logic applies to any durable asset: equipment, software, a website, even a training course that pays back over years. Amortization gives you the true per-unit or per-period cost so you can compare investments and set prices that actually recover what you spent.

## Context & Usage

Amortize whenever you evaluate a large purchase or need the real per-unit cost of something you bought once. Example: a photographer weighing a $6,000 camera spreads it across the ~1,200 shoots it will survive, seeing a $5-per-shoot cost that's easily covered by fees — a far more useful view than recoiling at the $6,000. Use amortized cost to price products (so each sale chips away at the upfront investment) and to compare a costly durable option against a cheaper one that wears out sooner. It keeps big one-time numbers from distorting decisions that should be judged over the asset's whole life.
