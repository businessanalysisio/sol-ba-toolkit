# Economically Valuable Skills

## Overview

Not every skill is worth money, and Kaufman's filter is blunt: a skill is economically valuable only to the degree it helps you create, market, sell, deliver, or manage the finances of something people will pay for. Being an excellent unicyclist is a fine skill but rarely an economically valuable one, because it maps to none of the five parts of a business unless you attach it to an offer someone buys. The practical consequence is that you do not need a business degree or exhaustive expertise; you need working competence across the handful of functions that actually move money. Kaufman also notes that skills compound — each one you build makes the others more valuable, so a person who can create a product and also sell it is worth far more than the sum of the two abilities in isolation.

## Context & Usage

Use this when planning your own development or building a team, to avoid sinking time into impressive-sounding capabilities that never touch revenue. If you are a strong engineer who cannot explain your work to a buyer, the highest-return investment is not deeper engineering but basic sales and marketing fluency, because that closes the gap between what you make and what you can capture. Score any prospective skill against the five parts: if it does not clearly serve one of them, treat it as a hobby, not a career investment.
