# Sunk Cost

## Overview

A Sunk Cost is money, time, or effort already spent that cannot be recovered — and, crucially, that should therefore be irrelevant to any forward-looking decision. Kaufman's point is that rational choices should weigh only future costs and benefits, yet humans systematically let unrecoverable past investment pull them into throwing good resources after bad: the "sunk cost fallacy." Because it feels like a waste to abandon something we've poured money or years into, we keep funding a failing project precisely because we've already funded it so much — which only deepens the loss. The correct question is never "how much have we already put in?" but "given where we are now, is continuing the best use of our remaining resources?" What's spent is gone regardless of what you choose next; letting it dictate the choice compounds the original mistake.

## Context & Usage

Invoke sunk-cost thinking when the urge to continue is driven by past investment rather than future prospects. Example: a company has spent $2 million on a project now clearly failing; the sunk-cost trap says "we can't quit after $2 million," while sound reasoning ignores the unrecoverable $2 million and asks only whether the next dollar is better spent finishing this or doing something else — often it isn't. Guard against it by evaluating decisions from the present forward, and treat prior spending as information, not obligation. Pairing this with opportunity cost sharpens it: the money left is what matters, and it should go to its best available use.
