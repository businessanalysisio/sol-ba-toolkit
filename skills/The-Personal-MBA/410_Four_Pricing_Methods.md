# Four Pricing Methods

## Overview

Kaufman identifies four ways to justify a price, each producing a different number for the same offer. Replacement cost asks what it would take to rebuild or reacquire the thing from scratch — the logic an insurer uses. Market comparison asks what similar items are selling for elsewhere, pricing by analogy to comparable deals. Discounted cash flow (net present value) values something by the future stream of cash it will generate, discounted back to today's money — the method for income-producing assets. Value comparison prices against the value the offer creates for this particular buyer, which is why it almost always yields the highest number: a service that saves a client $100,000 can command far more than its cost or its market average. The four methods are not rivals so much as four arguments you can reach for, and the price you can defend often depends on which frame the buyer accepts.

## Context & Usage

Use this when setting or defending a price, especially in a negotiation where the counterparty is anchoring on a low method. If a client benchmarks your fee against a cheap competitor (market comparison), you shift the conversation to value comparison by quantifying the outcome your work produces for them specifically. Real estate shows all four at once — a building can be priced by rebuild cost, by comparable sales down the street, by the rental income it throws off, or by what it is uniquely worth to a buyer who needs that exact location. Choose the method that both supports the number you want and is credible to the person paying; then present it as the natural way to think about the price.
