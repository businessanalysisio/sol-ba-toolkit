# Context

## Overview

A number on its own means almost nothing; it acquires meaning only in Context — against a comparison, a benchmark, a trend, or a baseline. Kaufman's point is that isolated figures create the illusion of insight while conveying little: "we got 500 signups this month" is unreadable until you know whether last month was 200 or 2,000, what you expected, and what it cost to get them. The same raw value can be triumph or catastrophe depending entirely on what you set it beside. A 3% conversion rate is strong for one channel and dismal for another; a $2M quarter is a record for one company and a collapse for another. Providing context — comparison over time, against a target, against a peer, or as a proportion of the whole — is what turns data into judgment, and stripping context is the easiest way to make a number mislead, honestly or otherwise.

## Context & Usage

Whenever a metric is presented or you present one, immediately ask "compared to what?" — versus prior periods, versus the goal, versus a relevant benchmark, versus what it took to achieve. Refuse to act on a standalone figure until it has at least one comparison anchoring it. Be alert to the manipulative uses: a big absolute number with no denominator ("thousands served"), a percentage with no base, a change with no starting point. This is why Ratio (555) is powerful — it builds context in by expressing one quantity relative to another — and why any KPI (549) needs a target or trend beside it to be actionable.
