# Confidence Interval

## Overview

When you estimate something from a sample, the honest answer is not a single number but a range — the Confidence Interval — within which the true value probably falls, together with how probable that is. Kaufman uses it to cure the false precision that measurement invites: a survey doesn't tell you "37% of customers prefer option A," it tells you the real figure is likely somewhere around 37%, plus or minus a margin that depends on how many people you asked. The smaller the sample and the messier the data, the wider that range, and reporting the point estimate alone hides exactly the uncertainty a decision-maker needs. A test showing 37% versus 34% is meaningless if each figure carries a plus-or-minus of six points, because the intervals overlap completely and the "difference" could easily be noise. The interval is what tells you whether a result is solid enough to act on.

## Context & Usage

Attach a range, even a rough one, to every estimate drawn from partial data, and be more skeptical the wider it is. Use it especially to judge whether an observed difference is real: two numbers whose confidence intervals overlap heavily haven't been shown to differ, no matter how different the midpoints look — the trap behind prematurely calling an A/B test with too few conversions. When a small sample forces a wide interval, the right response is often to gather more data before committing, not to pretend the point estimate is exact. This is the quantitative discipline that keeps Sampling (553) honest and reinforces Analytical Honesty (551) about what you actually know.
