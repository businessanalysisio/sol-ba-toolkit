# Alternative Types of Businesses

## Overview

Beyond the standard for-profit company sit several models that change who owns the business and what it optimizes for. Co-operatives are owned by members — workers, customers, or suppliers — with one-member-one-vote governance and profits shared as dividends among members. Social enterprises trade commercially but lock their purpose to a social or environmental mission, typically reinvesting most profit (forms such as the UK's Community Interest Company carry a statutory asset lock). Nonprofits and charities can run substantial trading arms but distribute nothing to owners. B-Corp certification, by contrast, is not a legal form: it is an audited standard a normal company can earn to signal verified social and environmental performance. The choice matters practically because it gates funding sources — grants and social investment open up, venture equity largely closes — and constrains how surpluses may be used.

## Context & Usage

Consider these forms when the founding motivation is mission, community ownership, or member benefit rather than owner return, or when the customer base itself should govern (buying groups, community shops, agricultural co-ops). Check conversion paths early: moving from a mission-locked form back to an ordinary company is difficult or impossible in many jurisdictions, so the decision is closer to one-way than choosing between sole trader and limited company.
