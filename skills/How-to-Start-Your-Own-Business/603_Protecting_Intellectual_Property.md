# Protecting Intellectual Property

## Overview

Four legal instruments protect four different kinds of creation, and matching the right instrument to the asset is the whole game. Trademarks protect identifiers — names, logos, slogans — through registration in specific classes of goods/services; they run in renewable terms (typically ten years) and can last forever, which is why the brand name should be trademark-searched before it is printed on anything. Patents protect inventions that are novel, involve an inventive step, and are industrially applicable; they buy roughly twenty years of monopoly in exchange for full public disclosure, cost serious money per territory, and are destroyed by disclosing the invention publicly before filing. Copyright protects original expression — text, code, designs, images, music — automatically on creation with no registration, generally lasting the author's life plus decades. Trade secrets protect anything valuable because it is confidential (recipes, processes, customer lists) for as long as secrecy is actually maintained — meaning NDAs before sensitive conversations and access controls inside the business. Two traps recur for founders: IP rights are territorial, so a home-country registration protects nothing abroad; and work paid for is not automatically owned — without a written assignment, a freelance designer keeps copyright in "your" logo.

## Context & Usage

Audit what the business actually owns at startup and before any fundraising or partnership: name and logo (trademark), product mechanisms (patent or trade secret), content and code (copyright, with contractor assignments in writing). File trademarks early and cheaply; take patent advice before any public demo or sale of a novel product, because that disclosure starts the clock. IP protection also runs defensively — search existing rights before launch so the business is not the infringer.
