# Balancing the Books

## Overview

Bookkeeping is the discipline of recording every money movement — sales, purchases, wages, loan payments — so that three statements can be produced and trusted: the profit-and-loss account (income minus expenses over a period), the balance sheet (what the business owns versus what it owes at a point in time), and the cash flow statement (actual money in and out). "Balancing" means these records reconcile: every bank statement line matches an entry in the books, invoices raised match income recorded, and the books explain where every penny came from and went. Two accounting bases exist — cash accounting records money when it moves, accrual accounting records it when it is earned or incurred — and the choice changes what profit looks like in any given month. Practical hygiene matters as much as method: keep every receipt and invoice (tax authorities typically require records to be retained for six to seven years), reconcile the bank account monthly rather than at year-end, and never let personal spending flow through business records. Most founders start with cloud accounting software plus a periodic accountant review; a full-time bookkeeper becomes worthwhile once transaction volume makes weekly entry a real job.

## Context & Usage

Set up the bookkeeping system before the first sale, not at the first tax deadline — retrofitting a year of receipts is where errors and penalties breed. Use the monthly reconciliation as an operating ritual: it surfaces unpaid invoices, creeping costs, and cash gaps early enough to act, and it feeds directly into budget tracking (596) and performance analysis (627). Accurate books are also the raw material for every later conversation with lenders, investors, and eventually buyers of the business.
