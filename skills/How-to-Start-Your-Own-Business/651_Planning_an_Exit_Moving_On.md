# Planning an Exit / Moving On

## Overview

An exit is the founder's planned route out of the business, and the choice of route shapes years of decisions before it happens. The main options trade off price, speed, and legacy: a trade sale to a competitor or strategic buyer usually pays the most but may dismantle what was built; a management buyout or sale to employees preserves culture and continuity but often at a lower, deferred price; passing to family keeps ownership in the bloodline but needs succession grooming and can strain relationships; a flotation (IPO) suits only large, fast-growing firms; and winding down — selling the assets and closing — is the floor option when no buyer values the business as a going concern. A fifth, increasingly common route is stepping back rather than out: hiring a manager (615) and keeping the business as an income-producing asset. Exit value is built, not found — recurring revenue, documented systems, a business that runs without the owner, and clean multi-year accounts all lift what a buyer will pay, and none can be created in the final month. The exit also has a personal dimension the numbers hide: many founders underestimate the loss of identity and routine, so planning what comes next (the next venture, retirement, a portfolio of interests) is part of the exit, not an afterthought.

## Context & Usage

Decide the intended exit route early — ideally at or near start-up — because it dictates legal structure, how much to reinvest versus extract, whether to take on investors, and how hard to reduce owner-dependence. Revisit the plan at each major inflection (a funding round, a big customer win, a health scare, an unsolicited offer), and give a chosen successor or buyer a multi-year runway rather than a scramble. When a specific sale becomes real, this decision hands off to the mechanics of Selling the Business (650); when the choice is to keep the business but leave day-to-day work, it hands off to Deciding Whether You Need a Manager (615).
