# Licensing

## Overview

Licensing turns intellectual property into a growth route without the licensor building anything: the owner of a brand, design, patent, process, or content (the licensor) grants another business (the licensee) the right to use it commercially, in exchange for royalties — typically a percentage of the licensee's sales of the licensed product, often with an upfront fee and a guaranteed annual minimum so the licensee cannot sit on the rights. The agreement's load-bearing clauses define scope precisely: which IP, for which products, in which territory, through which channels, for how long, and exclusively or not — exclusivity commands higher royalties but stakes everything on one partner's performance. Because the licensee manufactures, funds, and sells, licensing scales a product into markets and categories the owner could never reach alone with almost no capital; the price is control, which is why quality-control provisions (approval rights over product samples, brand usage rules, audit rights on royalty statements) separate licensing programs that build a brand from those that cheapen it. The whole model rests on enforceable IP — an unregistered trademark or unfiled patent (603) leaves nothing to license — and it cuts both ways: licensing *in* someone else's established brand or technology can accelerate your own product line for a royalty instead of years of brand-building. Licensing differs from franchising (574) in breadth: a franchise transfers an entire business system with ongoing operational control; a license transfers defined rights to specific IP and little else.

## Context & Usage

Consider licensing out when demand for the product or brand visibly exceeds your capacity or capital to serve it, or when adjacent categories (a design onto merchandise, a process into another industry) beckon outside your competence — and vet licensees on manufacturing quality and distribution reach, since their execution now carries your name. Take specialist legal advice on the agreement; royalty audits and termination-for-quality clauses matter most exactly when the relationship sours. As a growth option, weigh it in the 643 comparison: lowest capital requirement, lowest control.
