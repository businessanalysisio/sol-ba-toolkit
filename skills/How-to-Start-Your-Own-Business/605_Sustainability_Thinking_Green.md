# Sustainability ("Thinking Green")

## Overview

Green thinking in a small business operates on the triple bottom line — profit, people, planet — and the practical entry point is mapping where the business actually consumes and emits: energy in premises and equipment, materials and packaging, transport and delivery miles, waste streams, and the embedded footprint of what suppliers do upstream (usually the largest share, and the least visible). The reduce–reuse–recycle hierarchy is a cost tool as much as an ethical one: cutting packaging, energy, and waste removes spend permanently, and efficiency measures often pay back faster than marketing ever does. Beyond operations, sustainability becomes positioning — a growing customer segment selects on it and will pay a premium — but claims must be verifiable, because vague "eco-friendly" labels invite greenwashing accusations and, increasingly, regulatory action; third-party marks such as B Corp certification, Fairtrade, organic labels, or ISO 14001 exist precisely to substitute proof for assertion. A startup has one structural advantage here: designing green from day one (product materials, supplier choices, circular take-back models) is cheap, while retrofitting sustainability into an established operation is expensive.

## Context & Usage

Bake the assessment into early design decisions — sourcing (582), premises (581), packaging, and fulfillment (594) — rather than treating it as a later CSR project. Pick two or three measurable commitments (e.g., waste per order, renewable energy share, supplier standards) and report them honestly, including the unsolved parts; partial transparency is more credible than perfection claims. Revisit when scaling, since volume multiplies whatever footprint the model was born with.
