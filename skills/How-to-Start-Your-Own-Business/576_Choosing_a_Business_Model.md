# Choosing a Business Model

## Overview

A business model is the mechanism by which the venture creates value, delivers it, and captures some of it as revenue — the same product can support radically different models. Recurring patterns: one-off direct sales, subscription (predictable recurring revenue, judged by churn and lifetime value), freemium (free tier funds nothing until a percentage converts — often only 2–5%), marketplace or platform (take a commission on others' transactions, hard to start because both sides must arrive at once), licensing (rent out IP rather than sell product), razor-and-blades (cheap durable, profitable consumables), and advertising-funded audience models. Evaluating a candidate model comes down to three questions: how money comes in (revenue streams and timing), what it costs to serve each customer (cost structure and margin), and whether the model strengthens with scale or merely gets bigger.

## Context & Usage

Sketch the model on a one-page canvas after validating the customer problem but before detailed financial forecasts, because the model dictates the forecast's shape — subscription revenue builds slowly then compounds; project work spikes and dies. Deliberately test alternatives: many strong businesses come from attaching an unconventional model to a conventional offering (equipment leasing instead of sale, subscription boxes instead of retail). Revisit when unit economics refuse to work — often the model, not the product, is what needs changing.
