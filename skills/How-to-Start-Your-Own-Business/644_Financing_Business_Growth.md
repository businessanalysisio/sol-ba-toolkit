# Financing Business Growth

## Overview

Growth finance differs from startup finance in one decisive way: a trading history exists, which unlocks cheaper money — lenders and investors now price a real revenue line instead of a story. The menu, roughly in order of cost of capital: retained profits (free but slow, and the reason disciplined early businesses grow without asking anyone); bank term loans and revolving credit, now accessible on the strength of accounts (599) and forecasts; asset finance and leasing, which fund equipment against its own resale value; invoice finance/factoring, which advances typically 70–90% of unpaid invoices immediately — purpose-built for the growth trap where booming sales sit in debtors while wages fall due; and growth equity, selling a stake to fund expansion debt cannot responsibly carry, at the price of dilution and shared control. The matching principle governs which to use: fund short-term working-capital swings with short-term facilities, long-lived assets with loans amortized over the asset's life, and open-ended risky expansion with equity — mismatching (buying machinery on overdraft, funding payroll with a five-year loan) is how facilities get pulled at the worst moment. Size the ask from a cash flow forecast of the growth plan itself: the peak funding need usually lands months after the growth starts, because stock, hiring, and receivables all pay out before the new revenue pays in.

## Context & Usage

Arrange facilities before the growth push (643), while the numbers look calm — credit sought during a cash squeeze is expensive or unavailable. Model the overtrading scenario explicitly: what happens to cash if sales grow 50% faster than planned? Compare offers on total cost (rate, fees, covenants, personal guarantees) and revisit the startup-funding logic in 597 — the debt-versus-equity trade is the same, but the leverage has shifted in your favor.
