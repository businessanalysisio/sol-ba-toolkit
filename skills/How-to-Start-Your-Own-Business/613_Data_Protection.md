# Data Protection

## Overview

Any business that stores names, emails, payment details, or browsing data about identifiable people is a data controller with legal duties. Under GDPR-style regimes these duties are specific: collect data only for a stated purpose (with a lawful basis such as consent or contract), keep no more than needed for no longer than needed, secure it against breach, honor subject rights (access, correction, deletion), report serious breaches to the regulator within 72 hours, and in many jurisdictions register with the data-protection authority and pay a fee. Penalties scale to revenue — GDPR fines reach 4% of global turnover — but for a small business the bigger risks are breach cleanup costs and lost customer trust. Practical baseline: encrypt and password-protect customer records, limit staff access, vet third-party processors (email tools, payment providers) via written agreements, and publish a plain-language privacy policy.

## Context & Usage

Address data protection before the first customer record is collected — retrofitting consent and deletion mechanics onto a live mailing list is far harder than building them in. It resurfaces whenever a new tool touches personal data (CRM, analytics, email marketing) and when selling into new jurisdictions, since rules differ (GDPR in the EU/UK, CCPA in California, PDPA variants across Asia).
