# Managing Finances, Budgets, and Cash Flow

## Overview

The central distinction is cash versus profit: a business can be profitable on paper and still fail because money arrives after it must be paid out — customers on 60-day terms while suppliers, rent, and wages demand payment now. The core control is a rolling cash-flow forecast (13 weeks is the common horizon) projecting every expected inflow and outflow week by week, so the trough — the lowest predicted balance — is visible while there is still time to act on it. The budget sits alongside as the spending contract: planned income and costs by category, reviewed monthly against actuals, with each significant variance explained rather than absorbed. When cash tightens, the levers are known: invoice immediately and chase before due dates (debtor days is the metric to watch), negotiate longer supplier terms, take deposits or part-payment up front, trim stock that ties up money on shelves, and arrange a credit line before it is needed — banks lend most readily to businesses that do not urgently need it.

## Context & Usage

This is a weekly discipline, not an annual exercise: update the cash forecast every week in the first year, and reconcile budget to actuals monthly from day one. Escalate attention whenever the forecast shows the trough approaching the buffer, when growth accelerates (expansion consumes cash through stock and debtors even as sales rise), or when a single customer comes to dominate receivables.
