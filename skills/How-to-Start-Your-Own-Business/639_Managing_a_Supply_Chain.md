# Managing a Supply Chain

## Overview

A supply chain is the linked sequence from raw inputs through suppliers, production, and distribution to the customer's hands, and managing it means controlling three variables at every link: cost, quality, and reliability — you can rarely maximize all three, so decide which the customer promise actually depends on. Supplier selection weighs unit price against lead time, minimum order quantities, quality consistency, and financial stability; single-sourcing wins volume discounts and simpler relationships but concentrates risk, while dual-sourcing key inputs costs margin and buys resilience — the trade the pandemic taught every small importer. Inventory policy sits on the same tension: just-in-time ordering minimizes cash tied up in stock but breaks on any upstream delay, whereas safety stock plus defined reorder points (trigger level = expected demand during the lead time, plus buffer) absorbs shocks at the price of storage and working capital. Manage active suppliers with the same rigor as customers: agreed terms in writing, a handful of tracked measures — on-time-in-full delivery rate, defect rate, price movement — and a regular review conversation, because problems raised early are negotiations and problems raised late are crises. Distance amplifies everything: overseas sourcing adds weeks of lead time, currency exposure, customs, and communication lag to every one of these calculations.

## Context & Usage

This becomes a distinct discipline once the business outgrows ad-hoc ordering from its launch suppliers (582) — typically when stockouts or quality escapes start costing customers. Map the full chain including your suppliers' critical suppliers, mark the single points of failure, and fix the worst one per quarter. Supply chain review also belongs in crisis preparation (641) and precedes any growth push (643), since scaling sales against an unscalable supply base just manufactures backorders.
