# Setting Up a Franchise

## Overview

Franchising trades independence for a proven playbook: the franchisee pays an upfront franchise fee plus ongoing royalties (commonly 4–12% of gross revenue, often with an additional marketing levy of 1–4%) in exchange for the brand, operating manual, training, supply chain, and territory rights. The economics to scrutinize before signing: total investment including fit-out and working capital (usually several times the headline fee), realistic earnings evidence from existing franchisees rather than the franchisor's projections, contract term and renewal conditions, resale restrictions, and what happens on termination. Due diligence means reading the full franchise agreement and disclosure document with a specialist lawyer and interviewing current and former franchisees — especially ones who left. The trade-off is structural: lower failure risk than a from-scratch startup, but capped upside, mandated suppliers, and little room to deviate from the system.

## Context & Usage

Suits founders who want ownership with guard-rails — strong on execution and people management, less interested in inventing product or brand. Compare at least three systems in the same sector before choosing, and model cash flow through the royalty burden: a business that is viable independent can be marginal after 10% of top-line revenue leaves every month. Also relevant from the other side once your own concept works: franchising your format is a growth route (see Licensing, 649).
