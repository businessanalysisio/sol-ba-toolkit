# Attracting New Business

## Overview

Winning new customers at the growth stage is an economics problem before it is a creativity problem: every channel has a customer acquisition cost (total channel spend divided by customers it produced), and a channel only deserves budget while that cost sits comfortably below the lifetime value of the customers it brings — a common working rule is LTV at least three times CAC. That framing turns "do more marketing" into a portfolio decision across the options: referrals and word of mouth (cheapest and highest-converting, and engineerable — ask at the moment of delight, reward both sides); repeatable outbound (a defined prospect list, a sequenced approach, follow-up discipline — most B2B sales are lost to absent follow-up, not rejection); inbound content and search visibility (612), which compounds slowly; paid advertising (620), which scales instantly and stops the moment spending stops; partnerships and complementary businesses (625) that already own your audience's trust; and formal tenders for public or corporate contracts. Growth-stage attraction also usually means widening the funnel deliberately — a new segment, territory, or channel per the chosen strategy (643) — while instrumenting every source with a simple "how did you find us?" so per-channel CAC is a measured fact rather than a hunch (626).

## Context & Usage

Apply when the existing engine has been squeezed — conversion decent, existing customers buying fully — and growth requires genuinely new demand. Run the loop quarterly: rank channels by measured CAC and volume, double down on the best two, kill or fix the worst, and test exactly one new channel at a time with a set budget and deadline. Balance the hunt with retention (646); new logos that churn in months are expensive decoration.
