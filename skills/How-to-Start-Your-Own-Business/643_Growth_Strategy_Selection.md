# Growth Strategy Selection

## Overview

The Ansoff matrix organizes the four growth routes by how far each strays from what the business already knows, which is a proxy for risk. Market penetration — selling more of the current product to the current market through share gains, higher frequency, or better conversion — is the lowest-risk default and usually the first move, since it exploits assets you already have. Market development takes the proven product to new segments, territories, or channels; the product risk is zero but the market-knowledge risk is real (a proposition that wins at home can misfire abroad on price, culture, or regulation). Product development sells new products to the existing customer base, trading on relationships and distribution already built. Diversification — new product and new market simultaneously — compounds both unknowns and belongs only where a genuine bridge exists from current capabilities. Each route can be pursued organically (slower, cheaper, control retained) or by acquisition/partnership (faster, costlier, integration risk), and the honest precondition check comes first: is there demand headroom, does the operation have capacity (639, 638), and can the balance sheet fund the working-capital stretch — because growth consumes cash before it returns it, and overtrading kills profitable businesses.

## Context & Usage

Choose deliberately when the core business is stable and either plateauing or leaving demand unmet — not as a reflex to a good quarter. Score two or three candidate routes against demand evidence, capability fit, cash requirement, and downside if wrong; pick one, define what success looks like by when, and sequence the rest rather than running them in parallel. Fund the choice through 644 and protect the existing base while chasing the new (646).
