# Working with Other Businesses (strategic alliances)

## Overview

An alliance lets a small business borrow what it lacks — reach, credibility, capacity, or capability — without buying or building it. The common forms sit on a ladder of commitment: cross-referral arrangements (a wedding photographer and a florist exchange leads), co-marketing (shared campaigns, bundled offers, joint events splitting cost and audience), distribution or reseller deals (a larger partner carries your product to customers you cannot reach), and full joint ventures where both parties fund a shared undertaking. The selection test is complementarity without competition: the ideal partner serves the same customers with a different product and has as much to gain as you do — asymmetric benefit is the root cause of most dead alliances. Whatever the form, put the essentials in writing before starting: each party's contribution, how revenue or leads are shared, who owns jointly created assets and customer data, brand-use rules, and a clean exit clause, because the cheapest time to negotiate a breakup is before the partnership begins.

## Context & Usage

Look for alliances when a growth goal exceeds your own reach — entering a new customer segment, adding a complementary offer, filling capacity — and when the alternative (hiring, advertising, building) is slower or dearer. Pilot small with a single time-boxed campaign, measure referred revenue in both directions, and only deepen commitment once the exchange has proven roughly balanced.
