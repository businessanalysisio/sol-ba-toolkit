# Preparing for a Business Crisis

## Overview

Crisis preparation starts from a blunt inventory of the ways a small business actually dies or nearly dies: cash shocks (a major customer fails or pays months late), supply failure (a sole supplier stops), key-person loss (the founder or the one person who knows the system is suddenly unavailable), cyber incidents and data loss, physical disaster (fire, flood, break-in), and reputation events that go public faster than the response. For each scenario on that shortlist, a business continuity plan answers three questions in advance: what do we do in the first 24 hours, who does it, and what do we need pre-positioned to make it possible — backup supplier relationships already opened, off-site/cloud data backups actually tested by restoring them, insurance covers matched to the scenario (602), documented processes so operations survive an absent key person, and a cash reserve, with three to six months of fixed costs as the common survival benchmark. Communication is half of crisis response: decide now who speaks for the business, and hold to the pattern that works — acknowledge fast, state facts you are sure of, say what you are doing, never speculate — because silence and denial both read as guilt. Untested plans are fiction; a yearly hour of walking through one scenario ("the warehouse is inaccessible Monday morning — go") exposes the gaps cheaply.

## Context & Usage

Build the first version once real dependencies exist — staff, premises, key accounts — and refresh it on each new dependency. Prioritize by the risk register from 602: prepare deeply for the few scenarios scoring high on both likelihood and impact rather than shallowly for everything. In the event itself, the plan's job is to buy calm: decisions pre-made in peacetime are consistently better than the same decisions improvised at 2 a.m.
