# Retaining Talent

## Overview

Losing a good employee costs far more than the leaving party: recruitment fees, months of lost productivity while a replacement gets up to speed, and the knowledge that walks out the door — commonly estimated at half to twice the departing person's annual salary, and worse in a small team where one person may be the only holder of a skill. Retention drivers are well mapped and mostly not money: people stay for growth (new skills, widening responsibility, a visible path even in a flat company), autonomy and trust, a manager they respect — "people leave managers, not companies" — recognition that is specific and timely, and fair pay, which works as a hygiene factor: below-market pay pushes people out, but above-market pay cannot compensate for a bad boss or dead-end role. Small firms should play their natural advantages: breadth of role, direct impact, flexibility, and closeness to the founder — things corporates cannot match — rather than trying to out-bid corporate salaries. Watch the leading indicators (declining engagement, withdrawal from discussions, unused ideas) and treat every resignation as data: exit interviews, honestly conducted, reveal patterns while there is still a team left to fix them for.

## Context & Usage

Retention thinking starts at hiring — over-promising in the interview is pre-booking a resignation — and becomes urgent whenever a key person's departure would halt part of the business; map that exposure and spread critical knowledge deliberately. Review pay against market annually, and act on flight risk early: by the time someone announces an offer, the decision is usually already made.
