# Workplace Safety

## Overview

The core legal and practical tool is the risk assessment, a five-step loop: identify hazards (physical, chemical, electrical, fire, ergonomic, and increasingly psychological stress), identify who could be harmed and how — including visitors, customers, and contractors, not just staff — evaluate each risk and decide on controls, record the significant findings, and review whenever the work changes. Controls follow a hierarchy: eliminate the hazard first, substitute or engineer it away second, and rely on training, procedures, and protective equipment only as the last layer. Duties scale with headcount — many jurisdictions require a written health-and-safety policy once you employ around five people, and employer's liability insurance from the first hire — but the duty of care itself starts at employee one, and home-based or solo businesses still owe it to anyone their work affects. Keep an accident book and report serious incidents to the regulator where required; the record is both a legal shield and the data that shows where the next injury will come from. Safety pays operationally too: injuries convert directly into absence, insurance premiums, replacement costs, and — in a small team — the loss of a person nobody can cover.

## Context & Usage

Do the first risk assessment before opening, and redo it on every trigger: new premises, new equipment, new process, first employee, or an incident/near-miss. Make safety a routine agenda item rather than a binder — a five-minute hazard walk-round beats a perfect document nobody reads — and give staff an easy way to flag hazards, since the people doing the work see the risks first. This connects directly to establishing a healthy workplace (635) as the business grows.
