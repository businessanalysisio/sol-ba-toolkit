# Changing Strategic Direction

## Overview

A pivot is a deliberate change to a core element of the business model while keeping what has been learned and built, and the hard judgment is distinguishing the two signals that look identical up close: a temporary dip (seasonality, one lost account, an execution stumble) versus structural evidence the current direction has topped out — months of flat-or-falling sales despite competent effort, customers persistently using the product for something other than intended, a market visibly shrinking, or unit economics that never close no matter the volume. Pivots come in recognizable types, each preserving a different asset: change the customer segment (same product, buyers who value it more), the problem solved (same customers, different offer), the revenue model (one-off sales to subscription, product to service), the channel (direct to partner-led, offline to online), or narrow to the one feature customers actually pay for. The sunk-cost trap is the main enemy — money and years already invested are gone either way and justify nothing about the future — but its mirror image, pivoting at the first hard quarter before the original strategy had a fair test, wastes learning just as surely. De-risk the move like a launch: run the new direction as a bounded test alongside the old (584-style shadow testing), define the evidence that would confirm or kill it, and only then commit; and bring staff, investors, and key customers a clear narrative of why, because a pivot announced without reasons reads as panic.

## Context & Usage

Schedule the question rather than waiting for it: an annual strategy review against the original goals (571, 577) asks "knowing what we now know, would we start this business this way?" — and a persistent no is the trigger. In the decision itself, write down what is being kept (team, customer trust, capability, brand) and what is being abandoned, because a pivot that keeps nothing is actually a new startup and should be judged as one. Execution then becomes an organizational change problem (648).
