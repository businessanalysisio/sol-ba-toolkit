# Using a Customer Data System

## Overview

A customer data system — usually a CRM — replaces the founder's memory and scattered spreadsheets with a single record per customer: contact details, purchase history, interactions, complaints, and consent status. Its value comes from three uses. Segmentation: slicing the base by recency, frequency, and monetary value (RFM) to find who actually drives revenue, since a small share of customers typically accounts for a disproportionate share of sales. Pipeline management: tracking prospects through defined stages so follow-ups stop falling through the cracks. And service continuity: anyone answering the phone can see the full history, so the customer never repeats themselves. Two disciplines make or break it — data hygiene (one record per customer, entered at the point of contact, deduplicated regularly) and lawful basis (consent or legitimate interest recorded for every marketing use, with deletion honored on request under GDPR-style rules).

## Context & Usage

Adopt a system once customer volume exceeds what one person can reliably hold in their head — often around the 50–100 customer mark — and before a second salesperson joins, because migrating habits is harder than starting clean. Choose by matching features to actual workflow rather than buying the biggest suite; an unused CRM is a monthly fee plus a false sense of control.
