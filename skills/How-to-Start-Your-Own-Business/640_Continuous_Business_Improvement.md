# Continuous Business Improvement

## Overview

Continuous improvement replaces the occasional big overhaul with a permanent small-steps engine, and its working unit is the Plan–Do–Check–Act cycle: plan a specific change with a predicted result, run it small, check the measured outcome against the prediction, then either standardize the gain or discard the idea and keep the learning. The kaizen insight underneath is that many 1% improvements compound faster and safer than rare 30% transformations — and that the people closest to the work hold most of the improvement ideas, so the system must make suggesting easy and acting on suggestions visible, or the flow of ideas dries up after the first ignored one. Three inputs feed the pipeline: customer signals (complaints, reviews, returns, support questions), staff observations, and operating data (627); prioritize candidates on an impact-versus-effort grid and run only a few at a time, because ten simultaneous initiatives is how improvement programs die of fatigue. The step most small firms skip is locking in gains — without a written new standard (638), improved processes drift back to old habits within weeks, and the same problem gets "solved" annually. Baseline measurement before each change is non-negotiable: no before-number, no proof anything improved.

## Context & Usage

Install the rhythm once operations stabilize after launch — a short weekly or monthly review that picks one thing to improve is enough to start. Use it between crises, on the everyday friction that never becomes urgent: the error that gets redone weekly, the report nobody reads, the step that exists "because we've always done it." It pairs with process streamlining (638) as the maintenance loop that keeps streamlined processes from silting back up.
