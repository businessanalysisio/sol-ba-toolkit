# Managing and Resolving Conflict

## Overview

Not all conflict is a problem: task conflict — genuine disagreement about the work — sharpens decisions, while relationship conflict — friction between people — corrodes them, and the manager's first job is telling the two apart, because the first should be facilitated and the second defused. Resolution technique centers on separating positions from interests: the stated demands ("I need the Friday slot") usually mask underlying needs ("I can't do childcare pickups on the current rota") that admit more solutions than the demands do. The working sequence is to intervene early (avoided conflict does not evaporate; it recruits allies and resurfaces bigger), hear each party separately then together, keep the discussion on specific behaviors and their business impact rather than character, and land on a concrete agreement with a follow-up date. In a small business two special cases loom: founder–cofounder disputes, best pre-empted by written agreements on roles, equity, and deadlock resolution while relations are still good; and formal grievances, where a documented, even-handed procedure is both a legal protection and a fairness signal to everyone watching — and everyone is watching.

## Context & Usage

Use these skills at the first sign of friction — missed handovers, meetings gone silent, camps forming — rather than waiting for an explosion; early conversations are cheap, tribunals and cofounder splits are not. High-risk windows include rapid hiring, role changes, pay reviews, and any period of stress, and businesses mixing family or friendship with employment (585) should agree conflict ground rules before they are ever needed.
