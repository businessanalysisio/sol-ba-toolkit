# Problem Definition and Analysis

## Overview

Three problem-structuring exercises — problem discovery, problem framing, and problem scenarios and alternatives — that force a common answer to "What is the problem to be solved?" and "Are we solving the right problem?" before any solution is designed. Discovery uses the Cynefin framework (simple/obvious, complicated, complex, chaotic, disorder) to give decision-makers a "sense of place"; notably, the Guide says to start in the Complex domain, warning of failure risk if the complicated and simple problems are tackled first. Framing uses a 4W canvas — Who has the problem (is it validated, with proof)? What is its nature and evidence? Where does it arise in context? Why is it worth solving, and how acute is it? — often run as a timed reflect/synthesize exercise with dot voting. The output is a shared, well-articulated problem statement.

## Context & Usage

Used at the front of product work, because "too frequently, when teams are asked what problem their product solves, they immediately jump to talking about the solution." A clear problem definition pinpoints the target customer, lays out the roadmap, and prepares MVP/MMF/MMR/MMP planning. Insights feed in from personas, journey maps, empathy maps, interviews, observation, and data analysis; the scenarios-and-alternatives step then tabulates each problem scenario against the customer's current alternative and its value proposition — those alternatives are often the product's real competition. Its main limitation: a poor understanding of the customer degrades the quality of the entire definition.
