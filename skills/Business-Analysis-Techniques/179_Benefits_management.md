# Benefits management

## Overview

The whole-lifecycle discipline of making sure the benefits that justified an investment actually arrive, rather than treating the business case as a funding ritual. Its cycle: identify and structure the benefits (classifying each as, e.g., financial, quantifiable, measurable or observable, and stating its baseline and target measure); assign each benefit a named business owner — a manager who wants it and will answer for it, never the project team; plan realisation in a benefits plan that says when each benefit flows, what business changes (not just system delivery) must happen first, and how it will be measured; track through delivery, re-forecasting the case when scope changes; and review after implementation. The Cranfield benefits dependency network is the connecting model: IT enablers permit enabling changes, which permit business changes, which deliver benefits, which serve investment objectives — exposing that no benefit flows from technology alone.

## Context & Usage

Runs from business-case drafting to well after go-live, and reshapes analyst behaviour throughout: requirements gain a traceable line to the benefit they serve (a requirement serving no benefit is a cost), options are compared on benefit contribution, and scope-cut decisions consult the dependency network to see which benefits die with the cut. Common failures it exists to prevent: double-counted savings, benefits nobody owns, "softening" (quantified benefits quietly reworded as observable ones when targets look hard), and projects declared successful because they delivered on time rather than because anything improved.
