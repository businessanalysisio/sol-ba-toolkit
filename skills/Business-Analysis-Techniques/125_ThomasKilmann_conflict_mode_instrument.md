# Thomas–Kilmann conflict mode instrument

## Overview

Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann's 1974 framework (TKI) plots conflict-handling behaviour on two independent axes — assertiveness (concern for your own outcome) and cooperativeness (concern for the other party's outcome) — yielding five modes: competing (high assertive, low cooperative — pursue your position at the other's expense), accommodating (its mirror — yield to preserve the relationship), avoiding (low on both — sidestep or postpone), collaborating (high on both — dig for a solution that fully satisfies both parties), and compromising (mid-range on both — split the difference). None of the five is inherently correct; each fits particular conditions, e.g. competing suits genuine emergencies, avoiding suits trivial issues or cooling-off periods, collaborating suits high-stakes issues where both sets of concerns are too important to trade away.

## Context & Usage

Business analysts sit at the junction of stakeholders with incompatible objectives, so the TKI serves two purposes: diagnosing how a stakeholder habitually fights (and adjusting engagement tactics accordingly) and consciously choosing a mode rather than defaulting to temperament — most people over-use one or two modes. It informs stakeholder management planning, workshop facilitation when requirements conflict, and prioritisation negotiations where a reflexive compromise would produce a worse answer than a worked collaboration.
