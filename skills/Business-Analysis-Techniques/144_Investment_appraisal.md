# Investment appraisal

## Overview

Financial techniques for judging whether the cash flows of a proposed investment justify its outlay, recognising that money received later is worth less than money spent now. Three methods dominate. Payback period finds the point at which cumulative net benefits repay the initial cost — simple and intuitive, but blind to everything that happens after break-even. Discounted cash flow addresses this by dividing each future year's net cash flow by (1 + r)^n, where r is the organisation's discount (hurdle) rate and n the year number; summing the discounted flows gives the net present value (NPV), and a positive NPV means the investment beats the hurdle rate. Internal rate of return (IRR) inverts the calculation, finding the discount rate at which NPV falls to zero so projects can be ranked against each other and against the cost of capital.

## Context & Usage

Applied to the tangible cost and benefit figures assembled during cost-benefit analysis, usually to the standards and discount rate mandated by the finance function — the BA's role is to supply defensible inputs and interpret results, not to invent conventions. Payback suits risk-averse contexts and short technology lifecycles; NPV is the general-purpose comparator; IRR helps when competing projects have different sizes. All three inherit the quality of the estimates beneath them, so sensitivity analysis on the key assumptions belongs in any serious appraisal.
