# Kurt Lewin's model of organisational change

## Overview

Social psychologist Kurt Lewin's three-stage model (published 1947) treats an organisation as held stable by balanced driving and restraining forces, so change requires: unfreeze — destabilise the current equilibrium by building dissatisfaction with the status quo, presenting the case for change and reducing the restraining forces (Lewin argued weakening resistance beats piling on pressure, which merely raises tension); move (change) — transition to the new ways of working through involvement, training, and visible support while performance temporarily dips; refreeze — embed the new state so people do not drift back, by aligning measures, rewards, procedures and management behaviour with the new practice and celebrating its results. His companion technique, force field analysis, is the diagnostic used in the unfreeze stage to inventory the driving and restraining forces.

## Context & Usage

The foundational vocabulary for planning the people side of any business change a solution entails — analysts use it to check that a programme has deliberately designed all three stages, since the routine failure is skipping unfreezing (announcing a change to people who feel no need for it) or refreezing (declaring victory at go-live, after which old habits quietly return). In fast-moving contexts the "refreeze" idea is criticised as implying a permanence modern organisations never reach; the pragmatic reading is "stabilise until the next change".
