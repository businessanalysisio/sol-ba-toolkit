# Conscious competence model

## Overview

A four-rung learning ladder (usually credited to Noel Burch of Gordon Training International) describing how any skill is acquired: unconscious incompetence — you don't know what you don't know, so you feel no need to learn; conscious incompetence — you now see the gap, the uncomfortable but essential stage where motivation to learn is created; conscious competence — you can perform the skill correctly but only with deliberate concentration, following the steps one by one; unconscious competence — the skill is automatic and attention is free for higher-order concerns, the stage at which, paradoxically, people become poor teachers of the skill because they can no longer articulate what they do. Learners can also slide back a rung when conditions change.

## Context & Usage

Frames the training and support side of business change: users of a new process or system start unconsciously incompetent (hence awareness communication comes before training), pass through a conscious-incompetence dip that go-live plans must absorb with floor-walkers, quick reference cards and tolerance for slower throughput, and only reach fluent operation weeks later — which is when benefit measurement becomes fair. It equally warns the analyst about subject-matter experts: their expertise is unconsciously competent, so elicitation needs observation and protocol analysis, not just "talk me through what you do", to recover knowledge they can no longer verbalise.
