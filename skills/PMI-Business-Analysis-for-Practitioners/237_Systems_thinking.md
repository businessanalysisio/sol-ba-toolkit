# Systems thinking

## Overview

Seeing the organization as an interconnected system — people, processes, technology, incentives — so that local changes are evaluated by their whole-system effects. The canonical trap it prevents: speeding up the order-entry screen and thereby flooding a warehouse that was the real constraint, converting a visible queue into a worse invisible one. Habits of the systems thinker: always ask what is upstream and downstream of the process being changed, look for feedback loops (a metric that punishes reported defects will suppress reporting, not defects), distinguish the system's bottleneck from its noisiest component, and expect people to adapt to the new system in ways the requirements never anticipated. Boundaries are a modeling choice — drawing the scope circle too tight is how side effects get engineered in.

## Context & Usage

In Needs Assessment, systems thinking stops the situation statement from describing a symptom of a larger loop, and widens option identification beyond the department that raised the complaint. In Requirements Elicitation & Analysis, it powers scope models and context diagrams — every external interface is a place the system touches something that will push back. Traceability & Monitoring depends on it for impact analysis of change requests, and Solution Evaluation needs it to interpret results: a KPI improved locally may have been exported as cost to a neighboring team.
