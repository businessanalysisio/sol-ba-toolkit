# Estimation technique

## Overview

Estimating business analysis work is its own discipline, and Carkenord names why BAs get it wrong: elicitation effort is hostage to stakeholder availability, answer quality, and rework loops the BA does not control, so bottom-up task lists systematically omit the second interview, the third review cycle, and the workshop that had to be rescheduled twice. Her countermeasures: decompose the analysis plan (286) into estimable tasks rather than guessing at "requirements phase" as a lump; use three-point estimates — optimistic, most likely, pessimistic — so uncertainty is expressed instead of hidden, and a wide spread flags the tasks needing assumptions pinned down; calibrate against historicals from similar projects where any exist; time-box inherently unbounded activities like as-is analysis (283), converting "how long will it take?" into "how much is it worth spending?"; and state estimates with their assumptions attached ("three weeks, assuming SMEs give four hours weekly") so when the assumption dies, the estimate is visibly entitled to change. An estimate is a forecast; the moment it is repeated without its assumptions it becomes a commitment, usually someone else's.

## Context & Usage

Owed to the PM at planning time and again at each re-plan; re-estimate as elicitation reveals actual scope rather than defending the opening number. On agile teams the same thinking wears relative-sizing clothes — story points and planning poker — where the discussion provoked by divergent estimates is worth more than the number. Track your own actuals-versus-estimates across projects; a personal calibration history is the only known cure for optimism.
