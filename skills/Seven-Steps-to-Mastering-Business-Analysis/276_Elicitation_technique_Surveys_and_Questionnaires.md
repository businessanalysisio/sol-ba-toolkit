# Elicitation technique: Surveys and Questionnaires

## Overview

The only elicitation technique that scales to hundreds of respondents, which makes it the right tool for exactly two jobs: quantifying ("what percentage of branch staff use the export feature weekly?") and confirming at breadth what was discovered in depth elsewhere. Mechanics matter more here than anywhere: mostly closed questions with mutually exclusive answer options so results can be tallied; one or two open questions at most (each one multiplies analysis effort); neutral wording, because "how much do you like the new portal?" pre-buys its answer; and always a pilot with a handful of respondents before full distribution — a single ambiguous question silently corrupts every response. Carkenord's caution: surveys cannot ask follow-up questions, cannot read tone, and reward the opinionated minority who bother responding, so treat percentages from a 15% response rate with suspicion and never use a survey to discover requirements nobody has voiced yet.

## Context & Usage

Reach for it with large, dispersed user populations — field offices, external customers, a merged company's staff — for prioritization polls across many candidate features, and for before/after satisfaction baselines the business case can cite. Keep it short (completion drops per question), state the deadline and what will be done with results, and plan the analysis before sending: a question you do not know how you will tabulate should be cut.
