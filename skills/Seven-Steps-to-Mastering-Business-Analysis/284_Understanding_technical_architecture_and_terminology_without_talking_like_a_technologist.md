# Understanding technical architecture and terminology without "talking like a technologist

## Overview

Carkenord's "know your technical environment" asks for working literacy, not engineering: enough grasp of tiers and layers (presentation, application logic, data), databases versus files, interfaces and middleware, batch versus real-time processing, hosting and network basics, and the organization's specific platform stack to follow an architect's explanation, ask questions that earn respect ("is that interface event-driven or nightly batch, and what happens to orders created in between?"), and sense when a requirement is technically explosive before the estimate says so. The equally weighted second half of the skill is restraint in the other direction: requirements stay written in business language, and the BA who starts specifying tables and API calls has drifted into design — usurping the developer's job while abandoning the analyst's. The role Carkenord describes is translator at the boundary: able to render a technical constraint into business consequences ("real-time sync at that volume means either latency or cost — which does the business prefer?") and a business need into terms an architect can assess.

## Context & Usage

Exercise it in feasibility discussions, estimation reviews, non-functional requirements elicitation, and vendor evaluations where marketing architecture diagrams need decoding. Build the vocabulary deliberately — ask an architect to whiteboard the landscape for an hour, keep a personal glossary of the local acronyms. The failure modes are symmetric: too little literacy and developers route around you; too much display of it and business stakeholders stop talking to you in their own terms.
