# Elicitation technique: Document Analysis

## Overview

Mining what the organization has already written down: procedure manuals, training guides, blank and completed forms, system screens and reports, contracts, SLAs, help-desk tickets, and prior projects' requirements documents. Its unique economics — zero stakeholder hours consumed — make it Carkenord's recommended first move, and its outputs are concrete: forms yield data elements and business rules (every field, dropdown value, and signature line encodes one), reports reveal what the business measures, and old requirement docs reveal what was promised versus delivered. The trap is equally concrete: documents record intended practice, and often outdated intended practice at that. So document analysis never closes a requirement; it opens questions — the deliverable is as much an annotated question list for interviews and observation as it is draft requirements.

## Context & Usage

Ideal early, when stakeholders are scarce, when the subject-matter experts have left (legacy replacement projects live on this technique), and as preparation that makes later interviews sharper — asking an SME to explain the exceptions to a documented rule is far more productive than asking them to describe the process from scratch. Time-box it; a large document archive can absorb weeks for diminishing returns. Always note each document's date and owner: an unowned, undated procedure manual carries near-zero evidentiary weight.
